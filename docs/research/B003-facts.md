# B003 — fact-check pack: the plant that spans 180 km

**Channel:** Scale & Science · **Use:** research handoff for a faceless, phone-first long video.  
**Audit / web-access date:** **2026-11-18**. Dates in citations are publication, update, or measurement dates as identified; an undated web page is explicitly labelled.  
**Units:** `t` means metric tonnes; preserve “US short tons” where specified. `km` is a linear extent; `km²` and `ha` are areas. Approximation signs and inequality signs are part of the claim.  
**Confidence:** High = strong evidence for the specifically defined claim; Medium = defensible estimate / historical record with meaningful limitations; Low = unverified extreme, indirect age, or poorly constrained extrapolation. These are editorial evidence ratings, not calculated probabilities.

## 0. The headline verdict

**The supported reveal is: “A single seagrass clone spans at least 180 km in Shark Bay.”** The primary paper identifies *Posidonia australis* ramets in fragmented meadows as one widespread tetraploid genet. It does **not** describe a blade, stem, or continuously connected root that is 180 km long. Its approximately **200 km²** area estimate is tied to mapped *P. australis* meadow area, including an explicitly pre-2010/11-heatwave estimate used in the age calculation. Its **4500-year** age is a model-derived maximum in that calculation, not a measured birthday.[^shark2022]

**Recommended title wording:** **“This One Plant Spans 180 km”**. If retaining “Biggest Living Thing on Earth,” immediately explain that the proposed record is **by reported clone area**, under the **genet** definition of an individual. “The biggest” without a metric is not defensible: biomass, height, trunk volume, occupied area, and geographical span are different competitions. No reliable total biomass for the Shark Bay clone is established by its discovery paper.[^shark2022][^shermanNPS][^malheur2003]

**Important update:** An unrestricted “most widespread clone on Earth” claim needs revising. A Baltic *Fucus* seaweed clone is reported across **>600 km of coastline** in the accessible author manuscript associated with a **2023** genomic paper. This challenges a universal clone-span record, although it does not establish an occupied-area record or a connected >600-km body. Brown seaweed is also a different group from flowering seagrass.[^fucusMS][^fucus2023]

**Another important update:** Pando’s much-repeated **~16,000–80,000-year** estimate comes from the **2024** preprint. The **2026 eLife Reviewed Preprint** instead proposes **~12,000–37,000 years**, and its public assessment rates the evidence for clone age **incomplete**. Neither range is a settled age measurement.[^pando2024][^pando2026][^pandoReviews]

**Search conclusion:** Among the sources checked through the audit date, I did not verify a newer flowering-plant genet with a demonstrated occupied-area estimate exceeding the Shark Bay claim. That is a result of this literature check, not proof that no larger undiscovered or unindexed clone exists. Use **“reported as the largest known plant clone by area”**, with the discovery date and area caveat, rather than certifying an unconditional current world record.[^shark2022][^oceanica2026][^global2026]

## 1. “One organism”: the plain-language paragraph

A **genet** is the whole clone descended by vegetative growth from one original fertilized egg or seed; a **ramet** is one repeat unit, such as an aspen trunk or a seagrass shoot, that may eventually live independently. Pando’s trunks and the Shark Bay shoots can therefore count as one **genetic individual**, even when old roots or rhizomes between parts have died. They need not remain a single connected body, and small DNA changes acquired during growth do not automatically turn each part into a new genet. Conversely, a meadow, forest, reef, or underground fungal community can contain many unrelated genets, so sharing a species or looking alike is not enough to make it one organism. This video should say which definition it uses: **one clone descended from one origin**, rather than assuming that every part is still connected or exchanging nutrients today.[^clock2024][^shark2022][^pando2008]

For the siphonophore, use **“one integrated colonial animal.”** Its specialized multicellular units are **zooids**, not a random school of animals and not individual cells. The connected units specialize in jobs such as feeding, movement, and reproduction; describing that body as both “one” and “many” is a biological distinction, not a reason to arbitrarily disqualify it.[^siphMBA][^siph2022]

**Mass provenance:** A frequently linked original outreach source is Schmitt & Tatum’s **2008 USFS pamphlet**, *The Malheur National Forest: Location of the World’s Largest Living Organism*. **Definition:** The body is fungal mycelium and rhizomorphs, largely hidden in soil, roots, and wood; honey mushrooms are seasonal fruiting structures. The organism can infect and kill conifers. Show roots / hidden threads and a map of sample-derived extent, not an enormous aboveground mushroom. This is one of multiple distinct *Armillaria* genets in the area, not the entire forest’s fungus pooled together.[^malheur2003][^sciam2007][^malheurOPB]

**Taxonomy note:** *A. solidipes* appears as a name/synonym in some sources for this North American fungus. Keep **“*Armillaria ostoyae*, as named in the discovery paper”** to avoid suggesting that differently labelled reports discovered a second giant.[^malheur2003][^sciam2007]

**Hype flag — RED for an unconditional present record:** “The largest living thing on Earth” in older fungus coverage predates the seagrass discovery and fails to say which metric and definition it means. **“One of the largest known fungal individuals, by mapped area”** is defensible; **“heaviest organism, conclusively weighed”** is not.[^malheur2003][^malheurOPB][^shark2022]

### 3.8 The payoff — *Posidonia australis* clone, Shark Bay, Western Australia

**Best primary source:** Edgeloe et al., **online 2022-06-01**, issue **2022-06-08**, *Extensive polyploid clonality was a successful strategy for seagrass to expand into a newly submerged environment*, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* **289**, article **20220538**, DOI **10.1098/rspb.2022.0538**. The full paper was inspected through PMC, including methods, results, tables and the discussion’s age calculation.[^shark2022]

| ID | Exact source number and units | Confidence / what is disputed | Safe on-screen wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| PO-01 | **At least 180 km** in the abstract; discussion also describes **more than 180 km**, from White Island to Faure Sill in the eastern gulf | High/Medium for the reported spatial extent supported by genetic sampling; not a tape-measured blade or uninterrupted connection.[^shark2022] | **“One plant clone spans ≥180 km.”** |
| PO-02 | **Approximately 200 km²** of mapped *P. australis* meadows; explicitly **200 km² pre-2010/11 heatwave** used for age estimation | Medium for the authors’ estimated clone/meadow footprint. | PO-08 | Maximum SNP variation among polyploid profiles **4%**, with **2.8%** attributed to genotyping error; residual **1.2%** proposed as somatic variation | Medium for the single-lineage interpretation of those differences; the remaining variation is an inference, not proof of a totally mutation-free plant.[^shark2022] | **“One clone, with small genetic differences acquired over time.”** Do not say “every DNA letter was identical.” |
| PO-09 | Clone formed probably after inundation of Shark Bay **less than 8500 years ago** | Medium for the proposed environmental history / origin window. This is distinct from the 4500-year growth-model output.[^shark2022] | **“It spread as rising seas created new habitat.”** |
| PO-10 | Total mass / fresh biomass | **Not established by the discovery study.** Area cannot be converted to a whole-clone weight without a supported density and belowground inventory.[^shark2022] | **“Record claim: area, not known total weight.”** |

**How the DNA supports “one”:** The nine polyploid meadows had highly similar multilocus profiles, high heterozygosity, and no significant structure indicating separate populations in the clustering analysis. Small differences were interpreted as subclones within one widespread genet. The diploid Guischenault Point meadow was different and contained multiple genotypes. This is strong evidence for a huge clone **under the study’s genetic criteria**, not proof that all seagrass in Shark Bay is one plant.[^shark2022]

**Connection caveat is in the paper itself:** As clones age, rhizomes can die between genetically related shoots; the sampled meadows are fragmented. The study was not designed to trace continuous connected ramets. Do not draw one unbroken 180-km rhizome or claim that nutrients demonstrably travel between the distant ends today.[^shark2022]

**Polyploid mechanism:** Whole-genome duplication is established; a hybrid origin involving *P. australis* and an unidentified other *Posidonia* lineage is the authors’ **favoured hypothesis**, not a demonstrated parentage. Do not say “we know which two species fused” or that chromosome duplication was experimentally proven to cause the clone’s size.[^shark2022]

**Reproduction / survival:** Seagrasses are flowering plants with roots and rhizomes, not seaweed. The clone spreads vegetatively. Flowering has been observed across polyploid meadows, while the authors suggest **at least partial sterility**; do not assert that it never flowers, is absolutely incapable of seed production, or is immortal. It was affected by the **2010–2011** heatwave, with local recovery reported in some meadows, so “unaffected by climate change” is false.[^shark2022]

**Hype flag — GREEN for the genetic discovery, AMBER for the headline:** The finding is a peer-reviewed primary result, not merely press-release hype. **“A 180-km-long single blade,” “exactly 200 km² today,” “exactly 4500 years old,” “heaviest living thing,” and an unqualified universal clone-span record** go beyond what it establishes.[^shark2022][^fucusMS]

Not one uninterrupted thallus or measured length of all tissue.[^fucusMS] | **“A Baltic seaweed clone has been reported across >600 km of coastline.”** |
| Identification | *Fucus vesiculosus* complex; manuscript treats *F. radicans* within the expanding lineage | The manuscript acknowledges uncertain systematic relationships. Brown macroalgae are not flowering seagrasses.[^fucusMS][^fucus2023] | **“Baltic bladderwrack / Fucus seaweed clone.”** |

### 4.2 *Posidonia oceanica*, Mediterranean — older lineage claims, not a verified newer larger clone

**Best primary clone-size source:** Arnaud-Haond et al., **2012-02-01**, DOI **10.1371/journal.pone.0030454**. **New genomic check:** Pons, Castejón, Botigué & Terrados, Research Square preprint **2026-08-13**, DOI **10.21203/rs.3.rs-10582305/v1**. | Claim | Exact number / definition | Confidence and dispute | Safe wording |
|---|---|---|---|
| Largest verified distance in the 2012 study | Clone mates at locations **up to 15 km apart**; no shared multilocus genotype found for locations **more than 15 km apart** in that sampling | Medium/High under the microsatellite criteria; not all Mediterranean plants one genotype. | Extreme age scenario | **80,000–200,000 years** under exclusively continuous clonal growth for the far-separated samples | **Low as a true age.** The authors themselves call this scenario unlikely given sea-level history and favour growth plus fragment dispersal, implying less extreme ages.[^oceanica2012] | **“Ancient clones — exact age unknown.”** If mentioning the extreme, say **“An unlikely growth-only scenario suggested 80,000–200,000 years.”** |

**Other recent Posidonia checks:** A **2026-05-22** pan-Mediterranean study reports flowering observations from **442 sites** after the **2022** heatwaves; it measures reproduction, not the extent of one genet. A **2026-11-07** study of ***P. sinuosa*** reports one multilocus genotype shared between sites **~16 km apart**, while overall clonal richness was high; it is not a bigger Shark Bay clone. Do not confuse these species or the different kinds of “records.”[^oceanicaFlowers2026][^sinuosa2026]

### 4.3 New somatic clocks — a better method, not evidence that every large clone is ancient

**Primary source:** Yu et al., **2024-06-10**, *A somatic genetic clock for clonal species*, DOI **10.1038/s41559-024-02439-z**.[^clock2024]

* **Exact result:** Estimated ages among sampled *Zostera marina* genets reached **1403 years**, for the oldest sampled Finnish genet. The clock was calibrated using cultivated clones aged **4 and 17 years**. These are estimates from a calibrated molecular method, not tree-ring counts or a world age record.[^clock2024]
* **Confidence:** Medium/High for this specific methodological result and sample; conditional on calibration, somatic fixation dynamics, and adequate genomic data. It does not directly date *P. australis*, *P. oceanica*, or Pando.[^clock2024]
* **Key challenge:** Genet size alone is a poor universal age proxy because fragmentation or loss of ramets can leave an old genet small. The paper provides theoretical conditions for applying the clock; this is relevant to the public criticism of the Pando inference.[^clock2024][^pandoReviews]
* **Safe wording:** **“New genetic clocks can estimate clone age — but the method and sampling matter.”** Do not say “DNA now proves all giant clones’ ages.”[^clock2024][^pandoReviews]

### 4.4 Bahamas seagrass — a much bigger meadow, not a single organism

**Primary measurement chain:** Gallagher et al., **2022-11-01**, DOI **10.1038/s41467-022-33926-1**; Fu et al., **2023-12-13**, DOI **10.1038/s43247-023-01154-0**; Moritsch et al., **2025-05-16**, DOI **10.1038/s41598-025-01993-1**.[^bahamas2022][^bahamas2023][^bahamas2025]

* **Exact area:** The 2022 mapped-habitat result, used in the 2023 work, is **66,990–92,524 km²** across the Bahama Banks. The 2025 study uses seagrass maps to model sediment carbon accumulation; it is not genetic identification of a new one-plant clone.[^bahamas2022][^bahamas2023][^bahamas2025]
* **Confidence:** Medium/High for a very large, modelled/ground-truthed **ecosystem**; uncertainty remains around sparse seagrass and map coverage. High that it is **not presented as one genet**. The original survey describes several seagrass species, including *Thalassia*, *Syringodium*, *Halodule* and *Halophila*.[^bahamas2022]
* **Safe wording:** **“The Bahamas has a much larger seagrass ecosystem — made of many plants.”** If showing the range: **“66,990–92,524 km² — habitat estimate, not one clone.”**[^bahamas2022][^bahamas2023]
* **Hype flag:** Calling this area “one plant bigger than Shark Bay” is an ecosystem/individual category error, not a conclusion of those papers.[^bahamas2022][^bahamas2025]
